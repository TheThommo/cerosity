# Golf Rules & Formats — T0 Knowledge

## Basic Rules of Golf

Golf is played by striking a ball with a club from the teeing area into the hole on each of the 18 holes in as few strokes as possible. The player who completes the round in the fewest total strokes wins in stroke play; in match play, each hole is a separate contest.

You must play the ball as it lies — no improving your lie, line of play, or stance unless the Rules specifically allow it. You may not move, bend, or break anything growing or fixed except in taking your normal stance or making your swing. If your ball is unplayable, you always have the option to take relief under penalty.

Every stroke counts, including penalty strokes. A round begins at the first teeing area and ends when you hole out on the final green. You must use conforming clubs (maximum 14 in your bag) and a conforming ball. Touching or testing the surface of a bunker or penalty area before your stroke is now permitted under the 2019 modernised Rules, though deliberately testing conditions remains prohibited.

The Rules of Golf are jointly governed by the R&A and the USGA. The current edition took effect January 2023 and simplified many procedures including dropping from knee height, relaxed penalty area rules, and removal of penalties for accidentally moving a ball on the putting green.

*Source: R&A/USGA Rules of Golf, 2023 Edition*

## Stroke Play

Stroke play is the most common competitive format. Your total score across all 18 holes (or 36, 54, 72 in multi-round events) determines your finishing position. Every stroke and penalty counts toward a single cumulative total. The player with the lowest gross score wins.

In stroke play, you must hole out on every hole — there is no conceding putts. If you fail to hole out and do not correct the error before teeing off the next hole, you are disqualified. This makes every shot consequential and demands consistent mental focus across an entire round.

A key mental challenge in stroke play is that one disastrous hole can wreck your scorecard. The Red2Blue approach helps here: when you make a triple bogey, your Red Head screams that the round is ruined. Blue Head recognises it is one hole of eighteen. Process over outcome — commit to the next shot, not the last score.

Net stroke play adjusts gross scores using handicap strokes allocated to specific holes. This allows players of different abilities to compete fairly. Handicap strokes are received on the holes rated most difficult (highest stroke index).

*Source: R&A/USGA Rules of Golf, Rule 3.1, 2023 Edition*

## Match Play

Match play is golf's oldest competitive format: two sides compete hole by hole. Win a hole by completing it in fewer strokes than your opponent, and you go "1 up." Lose a hole and you go "1 down." Halve a hole by tying. The match ends when one side leads by more holes than remain — for example, "3 and 2" means three holes up with two to play.

Match play fundamentally changes strategy. You play the opponent, not the course. If your opponent is on the green in two, there is no need to attempt a risky recovery — take a safe bogey and move on. Concessions are allowed: you can concede a putt, a hole, or even the match at any time. Once conceded, it cannot be withdrawn.

Mentally, match play rewards aggressive recovery. A bad hole costs you one hole, not your entire round. Blue Head thrives here — the ability to reset after each hole is built into the format. Red Head players who carry frustration from a lost hole into the next tee often spiral.

The order of play matters: the side with the honour (won the previous hole) plays first from the tee. On the green, the ball farther from the hole is played first. There is no penalty for playing out of turn, but your opponent may ask you to replay the stroke.

*Source: R&A/USGA Rules of Golf, Rule 3.2, 2023 Edition*

## Penalty Areas

Under the 2019 modernised Rules, "water hazards" became "penalty areas." They are marked yellow (standard) or red (lateral) and can include any area the Committee designates — not just water. This change simplified relief procedures significantly.

When your ball enters a yellow penalty area, you have two relief options: (1) play from where you last played under stroke-and-distance (one penalty stroke), or (2) drop on the line between the hole and where your ball last crossed the edge of the penalty area, going back as far as you like (one penalty stroke).

Red penalty areas add a third option: lateral relief within two club-lengths of where the ball last crossed the edge, no nearer the hole (one penalty stroke).

You may now touch or move loose impediments and ground your club in a penalty area without penalty. You cannot, however, deliberately test the condition of the ground. If your ball is found and playable within the penalty area, you may play it as it lies with no penalty.

The mental key: penalty areas create decision points. Blue Head assesses options calmly. Red Head panics and either attempts a hero shot from water or takes poor relief. Your pre-shot routine should include a clear plan before every shot near a penalty area.

*Source: R&A/USGA Rules of Golf, Rule 17, 2023 Edition*

## Out of Bounds, Lost Ball, and Provisional Ball

Out of bounds (OB) is defined by white stakes, fences, or boundary lines. If your ball comes to rest OB, it is out of play. The penalty is stroke-and-distance: you must replay from the previous spot, adding one penalty stroke. This effectively costs two strokes (the lost shot plus the penalty).

A ball is lost if not found within three minutes of searching. The same stroke-and-distance penalty applies. To save time, play a provisional ball before going forward to search. Announce clearly: "I'm playing a provisional." If you find your original ball in bounds, the provisional is abandoned. If the original is lost or OB, the provisional becomes your ball in play.

Many clubs adopt a Local Rule (Model Local Rule E-5) allowing players to drop in the fairway near where the ball was lost or went OB, under a two-stroke penalty, instead of returning to the tee. This speeds pace of play in casual and club competition.

Mentally, OB and lost balls are among golf's most frustrating moments. Red Head floods in: anger, self-blame, wanting to smash the next drive. Blue Head uses the walk back to the tee (or the provisional ball routine) as a reset. The provisional ball itself is a smart mental tool — it keeps you moving forward rather than dwelling on disaster.

*Source: R&A/USGA Rules of Golf, Rules 14.6 and 18.1–18.3, 2023 Edition*

## Bunker Rules

Bunkers are specially prepared areas of sand designed as hazards. The 2019 Rules brought significant changes to bunker play. You may now remove loose impediments (leaves, stones, twigs) from a bunker without penalty. You may also touch the sand with your hand or club, provided you are not testing the condition of the sand or touching it in the area right behind or in front of the ball.

You still cannot ground your club in the sand directly behind or in front of the ball before making your stroke. This means no practice swings that touch the sand near your ball. In a practice swing, you may touch sand elsewhere in the bunker.

If your ball is unplayable in a bunker, you have three options: (1) stroke-and-distance back to where you last played (one penalty stroke); (2) drop in the bunker on the back-on-the-line relief, keeping the point where the ball lay between you and the hole (one penalty stroke); (3) for two penalty strokes, drop outside the bunker on that same back-on-the-line, going as far back as you wish.

The mental game in bunkers is about commitment. Many amateurs fear bunkers and tense up, creating tight grip pressure and poor contact. Blue Head treats a greenside bunker as a straightforward shot — open the face, commit to the swing, trust the technique. Red Head tightens, decelerates, and leaves the ball in the sand.

*Source: R&A/USGA Rules of Golf, Rule 12, 2023 Edition*
