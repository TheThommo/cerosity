# Golf Scoring Systems — T0 Knowledge

## Par System and Score Terminology

Par is the standard number of strokes an expert golfer is expected to need for a hole, including two putts. Par for each hole is set by its length: par 3 (up to ~250 yards), par 4 (~250–475 yards), par 5 (475+ yards). Most 18-hole courses have a par between 70 and 72.

Score terms relative to par: a birdie is one under par, an eagle is two under, an albatross (double eagle) is three under. A bogey is one over par, double bogey is two over. A hole-in-one (ace) means holing out from the tee in a single stroke — most commonly on par 3s but occasionally on short par 4s.

Understanding these terms matters for mental performance. When FLO asks about your round, knowing your scoring language helps communicate clearly. More importantly, Blue Head focuses on each shot independently rather than fixating on score relative to par. Red Head counts strokes obsessively — "I need birdie on each of the last three to break 80." Blue Head asks: "What does this next shot need?"

Course rating and slope rating measure difficulty. Course rating indicates the expected score for a scratch golfer; slope rating (55–155, standard 113) measures relative difficulty for bogey golfers compared to scratch. These feed into the World Handicap System calculations.

*Source: R&A/USGA Rules of Golf, 2023 Edition; WHS Rules of Handicapping, 2024*

## Handicap Index Basics

The World Handicap System (WHS), introduced in 2020, unified six previous systems into one global standard. Your Handicap Index represents your demonstrated ability: it is calculated from the best 8 of your last 20 score differentials.

A score differential = (113 / slope rating) × (adjusted gross score − course rating). The system automatically adjusts for abnormally high holes (Net Double Bogey cap) and exceptional scores (reduction applied when a score is 7+ strokes better than your Handicap Index).

To get a Handicap Index, submit a minimum of 54 holes (three 18-hole rounds or equivalent 9-hole scores) to your golf club through the national federation's platform. Your index updates overnight after each qualifying round.

For competition, convert your Handicap Index to a Course Handicap using the slope and rating of the course you're playing. This tells you how many strokes you receive. Strokes are allocated to holes by stroke index — the lower the stroke index, the harder the hole.

Mentally, your handicap should be a tool, not an identity. Red Head says "I'm an 18-handicapper, I can't do that." Blue Head says "My handicap shows where I am today. My process will move it." Track improvement through process goals (fairways hit, putts per round) rather than handicap alone.

*Source: R&A/USGA World Handicap System, Rules of Handicapping, 2024 Edition*

## Stableford Scoring

Stableford awards points based on your net score on each hole rather than counting total strokes. This format rewards aggressive play and reduces the damage of one disaster hole. Points are: net double bogey or worse = 0, net bogey = 1, net par = 2, net birdie = 3, net eagle = 4, net albatross = 5. Highest total points wins.

The format was invented by Dr Frank Stableford in 1931 at Wallasey Golf Club, England. It is now one of the most popular formats in club golf worldwide, particularly in the UK, Ireland, and Australia.

Stableford's mental benefit is clear: once you've reached zero points on a hole, you can pick up. There is no spiralling eight or nine on the card. This built-in safety net encourages risk-taking on reachable par 5s and short par 4s — you can swing aggressively knowing the downside is capped.

Blue Head loves Stableford: each hole is a fresh opportunity to accumulate points. Red Head hates picking up because it feels like quitting. Reframe: picking up is smart course management. Save energy and focus for the next hole where you can score.

Modified Stableford (used on some professional tours) adjusts the point scale — for example, birdie = 2, eagle = 5, bogey = −1, double bogey = −3 — creating higher variance and more exciting finishes.

*Source: R&A/USGA Rules of Golf, Rule 3.1b (Stableford), 2023 Edition*

## Common Rule Confusions

Several rules consistently trip up club golfers. Knowing these prevents unnecessary penalties and reduces Red Head moments caused by confusion during a round.

**Embedded ball:** You get free relief when your ball is embedded in its own pitch-mark in the general area (fairway, rough, paths). This was extended in 2019 — previously limited to closely mown areas. Drop within one club-length of the spot directly behind where the ball was embedded, no nearer the hole.

**Accidentally moving your ball:** On the putting green, if you or your opponent accidentally move your ball, replace it with no penalty. Elsewhere, if you accidentally cause your ball to move during search, replace it — also no penalty (changed in 2019). But if you move it while addressing it or making a practice swing off the green, that is still one penalty stroke.

**Wrong ball:** Playing a wrong ball in stroke play costs two penalty strokes and you must then correct the error by playing your original ball. Strokes made with the wrong ball do not count. In match play, playing a wrong ball loses the hole.

**Double hit:** If your club accidentally strikes the ball more than once during a stroke, it counts as one stroke with no penalty. This was a significant 2019 change — previously it was a two-stroke penalty.

**Ball moved by wind:** If wind moves your ball at rest, play it from its new position — no penalty, no replacement. But if you had already marked, lifted, and replaced the ball on the green and wind moves it, replace it on the marked spot.

*Source: R&A/USGA Rules of Golf, various Rules, 2023 Edition*
