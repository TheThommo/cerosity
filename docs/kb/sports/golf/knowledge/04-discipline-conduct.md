# Golf Discipline & Conduct — T0 Knowledge

## On-Course Etiquette and Spirit of the Game

Golf is unique among major sports: players are expected to call penalties on themselves. There is no referee following every group. Rule 1.2 states that all players are expected to play in the spirit of the game — acting with integrity, showing consideration, and playing at a good pace. This self-governance is foundational to golf's culture.

Key etiquette rules every golfer should know: repair your pitch marks on the green, rake bunkers after playing from them, replace or fill divots on the fairway, keep pace with the group ahead, and stand still and stay quiet when another player is addressing the ball. On the green, avoid stepping on another player's line to the hole.

Ready golf — playing when ready rather than strict order of play — is now encouraged in stroke play to improve pace. This means hitting when safe to do so, even if you are not technically farthest from the hole.

Mobile phones should be on silent. Cart paths should be followed where directed. Dress codes vary by club but generally require collared shirts and appropriate footwear.

The mental performance connection: etiquette reduces friction between playing partners. When everyone follows the code, Red Head triggers (being distracted by movement, noise, or slow partners) are minimised. Blue Head stays focused when the environment is respected.

*Source: R&A/USGA Rules of Golf, Rule 1.2 and Etiquette Section, 2023 Edition*

## Pace of Play and Managing Slow Rounds

Slow play is one of the biggest frustrations in golf. The R&A and USGA recommend an 18-hole round should take no longer than four hours for a fourball. Many clubs enforce pace of play policies with warnings and penalties for groups that fall behind.

Tips for maintaining pace: be ready to play when it is your turn, limit practice swings to one or two, begin reading your putt while others are putting, walk briskly between shots, and play a provisional ball when your ball may be lost or out of bounds rather than walking back later.

The 40-second rule applies in professional golf: each shot should be played within 40 seconds of it being your turn (or 50 seconds for the first player in the group to play). While club golfers rarely face formal time limits, adopting this mindset improves both pace and mental performance.

Slow play often triggers Red Head. Waiting around between shots lets anxiety build, internal chatter ramps up, and focus drifts to score rather than process. Blue Head handles slow rounds by using waiting time constructively — stretching, breathing, observing conditions — rather than stewing. If you are in a slow group, control what you can: your readiness, your routine, your breath. Accept what you cannot control and commit to the next shot when it comes.

*Source: R&A/USGA Pace of Play Manual; PGA Tour Rule 5.6, 2023*
