# Golf Mental Performance — T0 Knowledge

## The Pre-Shot Routine

A pre-shot routine is the single most important mental tool in golf. It creates a consistent entry point into Blue Head before every shot, regardless of the situation — whether it is a casual par 3 or the 72nd hole of a Major.

A complete routine has three phases. First, the decision phase: assess the lie, distance, wind, hazards, and commit to a specific target and shot shape. This is where strategy lives. Second, the rehearsal phase: take a practice swing that matches the shot you have committed to, feeling the tempo and contact. Third, the execution phase: address the ball, take one final look at the target, and go. No extra waggles, no second-guessing.

The routine should take the same amount of time every shot — typically 15 to 25 seconds from stepping into the ball to starting the swing. Consistency in timing prevents Red Head from hijacking the process. When you notice your routine getting longer, it means doubt is creeping in. Reset: step away, recommit, re-enter.

Tour professionals like Tiger Woods and Annika Sorenstam are famous for the precision of their routines. The routine is not superstition — it is a deliberate cognitive framework that narrows attention, reduces anxiety, and channels focus toward a single committed action. Blue Head lives inside the routine.

*Source: Rotella, Bob. "Golf is Not a Game of Perfect" (1995); R2B Core Methodology*

## The Three-Footer to Win — Putting Under Pressure

Short putts under pressure expose the gap between Red Head and Blue Head more starkly than any other shot in golf. A three-foot putt has a 95 percent make rate on the PGA Tour in normal conditions. In high-pressure moments — to win a match, close out a round, or save par after a recovery — that rate drops measurably.

Red Head on a pressure putt: grip tightens, breathing shallows, eyes dart between ball and hole, internal voice says "don't miss." The stroke becomes a stab rather than a stroke. Deceleration kills the putt.

Blue Head protocol for pressure putts: read the line once and commit. Pick a spot on your line one inch in front of the ball — roll the ball over that spot. Take two practice strokes matching the length you need. Address the ball, eyes to the spot, stroke through. Do not look up early.

The physical cue that matters most is grip pressure. On a scale of 1 to 10, your grip should be a 4 — firm enough to control the putter but soft enough for feel. Red Head pushes grip pressure to 7 or 8. Before addressing a pressure putt, consciously soften your hands.

Breath is your reset. One slow exhale before addressing the ball lowers your heart rate and re-engages Blue Head. The putt is the same stroke you have made thousands of times on the practice green. Trust it.

*Source: Pelz, Dave. "Putt Like the Pros" (2000); R2B Pressure Moment Framework*

## First Tee Nerves and Anxiety Management

First tee nerves affect every golfer — from beginners to Major winners. The physiological response is identical: elevated heart rate, shallow breathing, muscle tension, narrowed peripheral vision. This is Red Head activation — the body's fight-or-flight response treating a golf shot like a threat.

The key insight: nerves and excitement produce the same physical sensations. Blue Head reframes the experience. Instead of "I'm nervous" say "I'm ready." The adrenaline that makes your hands shake can also sharpen your focus and add yards to your drive — if you channel it.

Practical first tee routine: arrive at the tee with time to spare. Take three deep belly breaths — inhale for 4 counts, hold for 2, exhale for 6. Loosen your grip on the club. Pick a specific target — not "the fairway" but a tree, a bunker edge, a yardage marker. Commit to your shot shape. Run your standard pre-shot routine exactly as you would on the range.

Avoid the common Red Head trap of trying to impress playing partners with a perfect drive. The first hole is one of eighteen. A fairway with a 7-iron off the tee is worth more than a sliced driver into the trees. Blue Head picks the smart play, not the ego play.

Physical warm-up matters too. Hit balls on the range for 15 to 20 minutes before your round, starting with wedges and building to driver. A body that has already made golf swings is less susceptible to tension.

*Source: Gallwey, W. Timothy. "The Inner Game of Golf" (1998); R2B Activation Management*

## Back Nine Lead — Closing Out a Round

Leading a competition on the back nine is one of golf's hardest mental challenges. The scoreboard becomes visible, the maths becomes real, and Red Head starts calculating: "If I par in from here, I win by two." This projection into the future is the enemy of Blue Head execution.

The back nine lead triggers two Red Head patterns. Protective play: aiming for the centre of every green, taking irons off tees, avoiding any risk — which often leads to tentative swings and worse results. Score projection: mentally playing holes 14 through 18 before you have hit the shot in front of you.

Blue Head closes out rounds by staying in the present shot. Your commitment level should not change because you lead by three with four to play. The same process that built the lead will protect it. Hit to your targets, execute your routine, play one shot at a time.

If you feel your body tightening on the back nine, use the walk between shots to reset. Slow your walking pace slightly, drop your shoulders, unclench your jaw. These physical resets send signals to your nervous system that there is no threat.

Many club golfers struggle more with closing than with chasing. The reason is cognitive: when chasing, you have nothing to lose and Blue Head plays freely. When protecting a lead, Red Head adds weight to every shot. Recognise this pattern and name it: "I am in protection mode. I choose commitment mode instead."

*Source: Cohn, Patrick. "The Mental Game of Golf" (2002); R2B Commitment Model*

## Bunker Pressure — Committing to Sand Shots

Greenside bunker shots under pressure reveal commitment levels immediately. The swing mechanics are straightforward — open clubface, hit two inches behind the ball, accelerate through. But Red Head introduces doubt: what if I blade it over the green, what if I leave it in.

The physical marker of Red Head in a bunker is deceleration. The player starts the downswing but pulls back before impact, either leaving the ball in the sand or catching it thin. Deceleration is fear made visible in the swing.

Blue Head protocol for pressure bunkers: assess the lie (is the ball sitting up, plugged, or on a downslope), pick your landing spot on the green, commit to the length of swing you need, and accelerate through the sand. The splash of sand after the ball tells you the swing was committed.

A practical drill: in practice, draw a line in the sand two inches behind the ball. Your only job is to enter the sand on that line and exit past the ball. The ball gets in the way of the sand splash — you do not hit the ball directly. This reframing removes the fear of contact.

Grip pressure matters as much in bunkers as in putting. Soft hands allow the bounce of the wedge to work through the sand. Tight hands dig the leading edge and produce inconsistent results. Before each bunker shot, consciously soften your grip to a 4 out of 10.

*Source: Utley, Stan. "The Art of the Short Game" (2007); R2B Commitment in Pressure Moments*

## Slow Play Stress — Maintaining Focus Through Disruptions

Slow play is the most common external Red Head trigger in club golf. When the group ahead is slow and your group is waiting on every shot, frustration builds, focus scatters, and scores suffer. The problem is not the slow play itself — it is your response to it.

Red Head in a slow round: complaining to playing partners, rushing shots when the fairway finally clears (overcompensating for lost time), losing your pre-shot routine, checking your watch constantly, and allowing irritation to leak into your body as tension.

Blue Head treats slow play as a mental training ground. You cannot control the group ahead. You can control three things: your readiness (be ready to play the moment it is your turn), your routine (run it identically regardless of wait time), and your internal state (use waiting time for breathing, stretching, and course observation rather than frustration).

Practical technique: during a wait, pick a distant object and do a 30-second focus exercise. Look at the object with soft eyes, notice details, then shift attention to your breath. This trains the attention muscle that you will need for your next shot.

If you feel frustration rising, name it without judgment: "I notice I am frustrated." This labelling activates prefrontal cortex processing rather than amygdala-driven reactivity. Naming the emotion creates space between stimulus and response — the space where Blue Head lives.

*Source: Csikszentmihalyi, Mihaly. "Flow" (1990); R2B Attention Training*

## Recovery After a Double Bogey — The Mental Reset

How you respond to a bad hole defines your round more than the bad hole itself. A double bogey is two shots. The emotional spiral that follows can cost five or six. Red Head's response to a double bogey is predictable: anger, self-blame, mechanical tinkering, score catastrophising, and pressing on the next hole to get the shots back.

Blue Head recognises that in an 18-hole round, one double bogey is statistically normal for a mid-handicapper and not unusual for a single-figure player. The data shows that most rounds are decided by three or four key shots — and recovery shots after mistakes are often among them.

The reset protocol has three steps. First, physical: on the walk from the green to the next tee, take three deep breaths and consciously drop your shoulders. Release the club from your hands and shake them loose. Second, cognitive: park the score. You do not need to think about the double bogey again until your post-round review. The hole is over. Third, commitment: on the next tee, run your full pre-shot routine. Do not change your game plan. The temptation to get the shots back leads to reckless play and compounds the damage.

A useful reframe: you did not lose two shots. You played one difficult hole. The next holes are a fresh opportunity. Blue Head plays forward, not backward.

*Source: Rotella, Bob. "Golf is Not a Game of Perfect" (1995); R2B Recovery Protocol*
